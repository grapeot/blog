In [the previous article](/live-streaming-1-en.html), we looked at live streaming from Bilibili's perspective: how a platform uses zero-cost rules—level badges, monthly subscriptions, probability blind boxes, and split-screen PK battles—to turn street tips that normally cap out around a hundred yuan into a high-volume business. Yet one big question remained unanswered: the whales dropping tens or hundreds of thousands of RMB a night in these rooms are sharp, street-smart operators in real life. If all their money bought was a few seconds of cheap screen animations, nobody would keep throwing cash into the pot month after month.

Beneath that question sits an even deeper contradiction. One of the central takeaways from Part 1 was that platforms break through the tipping ceiling by pushing streamers to stop selling songs and start selling their own attention and companionship. Songs, however, can be copied infinitely—hundreds of people listening to the same track add zero marginal cost—whereas a flesh-and-blood streamer has only one mouth, one heart, and 24 hours in a day. How do you take human attention, which is scarce and exclusive by nature, sell it to dozens or even hundreds of people at once as a scalable business, and not drive the streamer insane in the process?

Whenever I drop into virtual singing rooms on Bilibili, I am purely an audio person: all I care about are diction, breath control, and pitch, and I am completely immune to cooing, scripted flattery, or "emotional value." After lurking for weeks in industry chat groups and listening to post-mortems shared by streamers and talent-agency (guild) operators, though, I gradually pieced together how the real people on and behind the stage untangle that knot.

## What Are Big Spenders Actually Buying?

To see how human attention gets sold at scale, we first have to understand what paying patrons are actually buying. Mention live-stream tipping to outsiders, and the knee-jerk image is lonely men paying young women to call them big brother and act cute. That demand certainly exists: some viewers lack warmth in everyday life or have little say at home or at work, and spending a few bucks in a stream for gentle greetings and sweet talk is a consensual trade on both sides. From a revenue standpoint, though, cooing into a mic doesn't bring in real money, because people paying for cheap flattery rarely spend much per transaction.

What, then, are the real big spenders paying for? Some go to the exact opposite extreme, buying a sense of dominance they can't get in real life. In live rooms I fairly often see patrons spend big specifically to mess with the streamer. Everyone might know a streamer is conservatory-trained and specializes in acoustic guitar R&B, yet a boss will walk in, drop an expensive gift, demand a children's song, and insist she sing it over and over with the backing track sped up. Across the broader live-streaming world, there are even stories of whales dropping massive gifts to make on-camera streamers do hundreds of squats in a row or bark like a dog into the microphone for hours. To patrons like that, the gift menu is simply a price list for buying another human being's unconditional obedience.

Making money through humiliation never goes very far, though—normal viewers aren't going to hang around a room watching someone do squats or bark to pass the time. What sustains long-term revenue in a healthy singing room is still ordinary high-income listeners who genuinely enjoy music. Watch closely, however, and you'll notice they aren't really paying for the songs themselves; they are paying for privileges and favoritism that no music streaming app can offer.

When you listen to a track on Spotify or Apple Music, no matter how flawlessly it was recorded in the studio, the song has nothing to do with you. In a live room, when you pay to request a song or drop a heavy gift during a PK battle's scoring window, everything the room sings and talks about for the next several minutes revolves around you—you effectively take the director's chair for everyone watching.

Alongside directorial control comes public prestige in front of a crowd. In everyday life, even a well-off professional or small business owner rarely gets to stand under a spotlight to a round of applause. In a live room, the moment a big gift lands, flashy animations fill the screen, the streamer calls out your name loud and clear in thanks, and the entire chat floods with cheers. Nor does it end when the broadcast stops: after going offline, streamers routinely screenshot the night's gift leaderboard and post a public thank-you note on their profile feed—a ritual insiders call *xiebang* (thanking the board)—so anyone scrolling past the next day can see exactly who backed her, delivering full public face both on and off the air.

Even more compelling than control or prestige is the feeling that someone genuinely kept you in mind. I once witnessed a moment like this firsthand while listening in a singing room. One day a listener casually mentioned an obscure song in chat; the streamer glanced at it, said she didn't know how to sing it yet, and the moment passed. Some time later, the same listener wandered back into the room. Not only did the streamer recognize his handle right away, she told him she had practiced that obscure song in private until she had it down, pulled up the backing track on the spot, and sang it for him. The second the song ended, he didn't hesitate—he immediately bought a ¥198/month Captain membership on Bilibili's monthly subscription tier. Even watching from the sidelines, I felt that ¥198 was money well spent: what it bought was the sincerity of having an offhand remark remembered and quietly fulfilled weeks later.

Strip away the surface differences—whether it is dominance, live directorial control, public face in front of a crowd, or the warmth of being remembered—and every single one of them comes down to the same thing. What whales pay big money for is the attention and sense of personal care directed at them by the real person on the other side of the screen.

## How Can One Person Revolve Around Dozens of Patrons at Once?

The fact that patrons are buying real human attention immediately caps how far the business can scale: one person's attention is inherently exclusive, making it hard to grow revenue. Outsiders often assume a streamer has it made if she gets lucky and latches onto a single wealthy patron—known in the scene as a "big brother." In reality, the exact opposite happens. When a room has a single #1 whale whose spending dwarfs everyone else's by a mile, that room's revenue hits a ceiling fast. The logic is simple: a whale pays for status in front of the crowd and special favor from the streamer. Once he sits unchallenged at the top of the leaderboard with no rival in sight and the streamer already revolving around him, he has no reason to keep burning cash to prove himself. Conversely, if a streamer tries to make a living off small tips from dozens of smaller patrons, a broadcast is only a few hours long—her voice and attention can't be split that many ways, and anyone who feels slighted will walk.

To break past that ceiling, the industry worked out one playbook onstage and another backstage. Onstage, the move is staging male-rival or female-rival bidding wars. Getting a dominant whale who has already plateaued to open his wallet again usually takes nothing more than a second deep-pocketed newcomer showing up. As soon as a generous new patron appears, an experienced streamer will deliberately cool things down just a touch with the incumbent whale—putting the newcomer's name first in the post-stream thank-you post, say, or chatting a bit more warmly when answering the newcomer's comments. Seeing his monopoly slip, the incumbent immediately rises to the bait. What used to be one-sided patronage turns overnight into two people locking horns for pride in front of a packed room: you drop a big gift, so I have to slam down an even pricier one to top you. Once tempers and egos heat up, the combined amount the two of them spend often doubles what the room used to pull in.

Backstage, the move is *silian*—adding patrons on WeChat to maintain relationships privately outside the stream. If you pause to think about it, there is nothing natural about this arrangement. Imagine listening to a street musician busking on the sidewalk, loving the performance, and dropping ¥200 (~$28) into the guitar case; there is zero chance that tip gets you the singer's personal WeChat. As live streaming grew more cutthroat over the past few years and a few broadcast hours proved too small a pie to slice up, streamers pushed the battlefield into after-hours WeChat. In many singing rooms today, spending around ¥200 to hop on as a Captain is enough to add the streamer's work WeChat, unlocking mass-sent "good morning" and "good night" texts along with everyday photos of lunch or pets. Through broadcast messages and casual small talk on WeChat, a streamer can keep dozens or even hundreds of patrons feeling simultaneously pampered outside broadcast hours, each convinced they hold a special place.

## The ¥300k One-Person Company and Emotional Calluses

Stoking rivalry onstage and maintaining hundreds of WeChat contacts offstage certainly blows the revenue ceiling open for a single room. How much can a streamer who pulls this off actually make—and at what cost?

When I first encountered this world, I figured streaming was much like a sales job: memorize a standard set of industry pitches and you're ready to clock in. Watching longer made it clear that every mature live room is really an independently run one-person company (OPC): while live on air, song selection, singing, crowd control, and regular-patron maintenance all rest on the streamer's own shoulders (we will come back to backend roles like agency operations in a later article). The wealth gap across these one-person companies, however, is staggering. There are only so many deep-pocketed whales across the platform, and most cluster in top rooms. [A statistical report from the China Association of Performing Arts](http://sc.people.com.cn/n2/2023/1016/c345167-40604776.html) found that even among streamers who rely on live streaming as their primary source of income, 95.2% earn under ¥5,000 (~$700) a month; for a newcomer, taking home ¥3,000 to ¥5,000 a month is already considered a decent start. Anyone serious about sticking with virtual streaming also has to pay artists and riggers several thousand to tens of thousands of RMB out of pocket upfront for a custom 2D model—known in the circle as a private rig. Anxious newcomers sometimes sign with so-called guaranteed-base agencies instead. Those agencies cast a wide net, offering batches of recruits a meager first-month guarantee of a few hundred or a thousand yuan; miss the revenue target and the guarantee is cut immediately, while trying to terminate the contract locks the streamer out of broadcasting for the next several months.

On the flip side, for those who survive the cold start and rely on steady vocals, a deep songbook, and diligent off-stream upkeep to build a base of several dozen monthly Grand Voyage subscribers—topped off with regular gifts from whales—the financial payoff is substantial. Established virtual singing streamers on Bilibili routinely generate ¥35,000 to ¥60,000 in gross monthly revenue; after Bilibili takes its 50% platform cut, net take-home pay averages around ¥25,000 a month, or roughly ¥300,000 (~$41,500) a year. Sitting at home singing and chatting without a rush-hour commute, the physical workload really isn't heavy at all.

Making ¥300,000 a year without leaving home sounds like a dream job. Streamers who climb to that rung, however, quickly run headlong into the business's second problem: the human mind isn't built to take the strain. Because a streamer gets paid to supply emotional value, dealing with emotionally dependent regulars means privately listening to their everyday gripes and work frustrations, while replying a little too slowly can set off jealous tantrums from boyfriend-minded fans. When dealing with controlling whales who frame bossiness as "for your own good"—dictating stream style or dumping dark moods late at night—she usually has to smile and swallow it to protect room revenue. Add dozens or hundreds of fans let into her work WeChat at a ¥200 threshold, with notifications buzzing nonstop long after the mic goes off, and the last shred of personal space disappears.

One veteran agency operator summed up a pattern he sees all the time: among streamers who broadcast full-time for six months to a year or more, many experience a noticeable warp in their views on relationships and money. Once they switch off the mic and return to offline life, they often shy away from normal socializing and forget how to hold an ordinary conversation. Hard-earned income rarely stays in the bank either—much of it gets burned right back on paid gaming companions or tipping other streamers, paying someone else to pamper them just to decompress. Over time, bombarded by sweet talk and emotional demands on both sides of the screen, they grow increasingly numb. That numbness is the brain's self-defense mechanism: just as manual labor builds calluses on your palms, relentless emotional wear and tear builds a thick layer of emotional calluses on a streamer's mind. Without turning a bit dull inside, an ordinary person's mental defenses simply wouldn't survive the daily grind of negative energy.

## How Do Streamers Protect Themselves?

At this point you might wonder: faced with all that emotional drain and boundary-pushing, are streamers completely helpless? Not entirely. Humans are social animals, and whenever a crowd gathers inside a live room, viewers and the streamer are quietly testing status and boundaries with every interaction.

From what I have observed, a streamer's first line of defense is real craft. When a performer has undeniable chops—solid vocal technique, for instance—the moment she opens her mouth she commands the room's atmosphere, because the audience is actually there for the music. Inside a setting built around serious listening, people care about how they look in front of others, and nobody wants to embarrass themselves by blurting out sleazy or out-of-line remarks in public chat. Flip the setup around—say, a streamer with no real craft anchoring the room who relies purely on banter, known in the scene as a *zatan* (chatting) streamer—and without a body of work holding the floor, viewers easily push their luck, probing her with bizarre or transgressive requests and forcing her to develop ironclad crowd-control skills just to keep the room in check.

Even strong vocals and sharp crowd control, however, can't solve two deeper vulnerabilities. First, nobody can stay poised forever on marathon schedules—some streamers broadcast 12 hours a day, and physical exhaustion eventually shows. Second, once interactions move into the one-on-one WeChat messages mentioned earlier, the watchful eyes of the public room disappear, and some people lose all sense of boundaries. All it takes is one moment of losing composure on camera, or snapping late at night on WeChat and blocking a paying patron, and a painstakingly built income stream evaporates on the spot.

To cope with those two vulnerabilities, the industry gradually settled on ways to separate the public persona from the real human being behind it. Start with the front of the house: virtual singing streamers never show their real faces, appearing instead as a 2D anime character driven via an ordinary webcam that tracks blinks and mouth movements—what insiders call a *pitao* (avatar rig). Truth be told, most women who put on a 2D rig to become virtual streamers aren't initially doing it to protect their mental health. If a young woman sings beautifully and also happens to look like a movie star, chances are she went straight to on-camera showrooms where showing her face pays immediately, rather than spending thousands of yuan out of pocket commissioning a virtual model. Choosing the virtual route usually means physical appearance isn't her strongest card.

By a happy accident, though, over the grind of high-intensity daily streaming, that 2D rig ends up serving as remarkably effective protection. On a real-face stream, pulling several late nights in a row leaves you looking visibly drained, and if a disgusting comment scrolls past and even a one-second flash of annoyance crosses your face, viewers spot it instantly and the warm impression shatters. Behind a 2D avatar, the webcam only picks up basic movements like opening your mouth, blinking, and tilting your head. Even if the real person behind the monitor is slumped in her chair exhausted or rolling her eyes at an obnoxious comment, what appears on screen is still the same bright, energetic anime girl. Better yet, hiding a real face behind a great voice leaves plenty of room for listeners' imaginations: everyone can mentally fill in their own ideal partner around her voice, making it far easier to sustain that "more than friends, not quite lovers" illusion over the long haul.

An avatar shields the streamer onstage, of course, but what about the hundred-plus contacts sitting in her work WeChat? That brings us to the backstage half of the separation: handing private WeChat maintenance over to someone else. Word has circulated in the community for years about solo streamers outsourcing their work WeChat to commission-based male operators; inside scaled entertainment guilds, meanwhile, the practice was industrialized into standardized ghost-chatting factories long ago. Years back, [a 2022 CCTV 3·15 Gala exposé](https://tv.cctv.com/2022/03/15/VIDE0yWCYpDPEd1x8t2FtbSN220315.shtml) and [follow-up investigative reporting by The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_17142742) laid out exactly how this backend operation works. Guilds named in the broadcasts, such as Juxiang Huyu, recruited whole teams of young men—known in the trade as "chatters"—whose daily job was logging into female streamers' work WeChat accounts. Armed with pre-recorded "good morning" and "good night" voice clips and everyday photos supplied by the streamer, these young men used cute profile pictures to trade sweet nothings and heartfelt essays with male leaderboard fans, coaxing them to keep spending in the live room.

Most people watching those news reports understandably condemn the agencies for fraud. Look at the arrangement through an operational lens, however, and whether it is a large guild hiring male chatters in bulk or a solo streamer paying a male operator to run her WeChat, alongside tirelessly sweet-talking dozens of patrons into tipping there is a very practical reason at work: shielding female streamers from malicious harassment. Once rooms competed the threshold for WeChat access down to around ¥200, all sorts of people got through the door, and private chat windows regularly light up with explicit vulgarities and sleazy abuse. Put yourself in the streamer's shoes: if a woman is answering messages on her phone late at night, no matter how composed she usually is, reading graphic sexual insults aimed straight at her is going to leave any normal person disgusted and furious. Lose a night's sleep seething over it, and her voice and mood for the next day's stream suffer; lose her temper and block the guy, and a revenue stream gets cut off. Swap in a young guy working on commission behind a desktop monitor, and the dynamic flips completely: the guy on the other end can send the filthiest jokes or nastiest insults imaginable, and a grown man reading the screen won't take a word of it personally. It doesn't faze him in the slightest—he can calmly type back a gentle, polite greeting without blinking and keep the patron happily paying.

## Closing Thoughts

Looking back at the question we started with, the central contradiction of this business model comes into sharp focus. Whales keep spending big money in live rooms because they want genuine human attention and care that no music app can provide; yet a single person's time and emotional bandwidth are finite, and the moment a streamer or guild tries to make the business large and durable, relying on one flesh-and-blood human to absorb everything directly simply doesn't scale. Everyone ends up layering armor onto the human at the center: staging rivalries onstage to multiply spending, slipping on a 2D avatar to hide exhaustion, mass-messaging WeChat contacts backstage to maintain daily warmth, and eventually putting unflappable male operators behind the keyboard to handle private chats. A business built on selling authentic human care ends up pushing the actual human further and further backstage, relying on a semi-industrial pipeline just to stay afloat.

Still, much of what we have unpacked across these first two articles comes from reasoning through platform rules and listening to insiders' post-mortems. Out in real live rooms, plenty of questions can't be settled by naked-eye observation alone: in an actual singing room, what share of people walking through the door are silent lurkers who never say a word? How much of total revenue really comes from whales versus ordinary listeners? Do common streamer tactics like shouting non-stop welcomes or handing out red packets actually retain viewers? Fortunately, chat messages, room entries, and gift logs on Bilibili live streams are almost entirely public. Across two collection phases, we gathered over 1,000 hours of real data spanning several hundred live rooms; in the next article, we will put that dataset under the microscope to see what the underlying commercial logic actually looks like.
